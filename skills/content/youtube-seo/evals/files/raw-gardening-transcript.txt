People overthink their first vegetable garden, and then they give up in July when everything dies. I want to show you the version that survives. This is the exact plan I give everyone who asks me how to start.

Start smaller than feels reasonable. Most beginners plant a garden the size of a car, and then it turns into a full-time watering job they resent. A four by eight raised bed is plenty for a first year. You can grow enough tomatoes, peppers, and lettuce for a small family out of one of those, and you can actually keep up with it.

Put the bed where it gets at least six hours of direct sun. This is the number one reason gardens fail. People plant near the house under an overhang or next to the fence because it's convenient, and then everything grows leggy and weak. Watch your yard for a couple of days before you build anything, and mark where the sun actually lands.

Fill the bed right the first time, because fixing soil later is miserable. A mix of about half quality garden soil and half compost, filled to the top, will feed your plants all season. Cheap fill dirt is the false economy everyone falls for. Your plants will eat through it by June and then stall.

Plant what you'll actually eat, not what looks pretty in the catalog. If you don't eat zucchini, don't grow zucchini. You'll have four plants making twenty pounds of squash nobody wants. A beginner friendly lineup is tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, and herbs, because those give you something almost every week and they're hard to kill if you water them right.

Watering is where people either win or lose. Deep and less often beats shallow and daily. The goal is to soak the root zone, not just wet the surface. An inch of water a week, delivered all at once, makes roots grow down and strong. A sprinkle every evening makes roots lazy and shallow, and then any heat wave kills the plant.

Mulch like your life depends on it. Four inches of straw or shredded leaves on top of the soil keeps moisture in, keeps weeds down, and keeps the soil cool. Gardens that get mulched in June are the ones still producing in September.

Expect losses and don't take them personally. Slugs will eat something. A transplant will die for no reason. That's gardening. Plant a little extra, treat it as a learning season, and the second year will be dramatically better because you'll know your specific yard now.
